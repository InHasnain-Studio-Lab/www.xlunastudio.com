Windows can show a slideshow or a daily photo on your desktop, but it has no built in way to play an animated wallpaper. Every live wallpaper you have seen on Windows 11 comes from an app that draws a window behind your desktop icons and keeps painting it. That is why some feel effortless and others make a laptop fan spin up: it all depends on how that app draws and when it stops.

Two kinds of live wallpaper

Almost every live wallpaper is one of two things, and they cost your PC in very different ways.

  • A looping video. The app plays a video file behind your icons. Your graphics hardware has a dedicated video decoder, so a well made video wallpaper is cheap to play. The cost grows with resolution: a 4K loop on a 4K screen asks for far more than a 1080p loop.
  • A scene drawn live. The app calculates every frame as it goes: particles, a turning planet, shapes that react to the mouse or to your PC's activity. This can look far richer than a video and can respond to what you do, but each frame is real work for the processor or graphics card.

Neither kind is wrong. A video loop is the lighter choice for an older laptop; a live scene is the richer choice for a desktop with headroom. What matters most is whether the app is careful about when it draws.

What a moving desktop actually costs

Open Task Manager, go to Processes, and watch the CPU and GPU columns for the wallpaper app with your desktop showing. A well behaved live wallpaper sits in the low single digits on a modern PC. If it stays high, or keeps working while a game is running, it is drawing frames nobody can see.

On a laptop the real cost is battery. A wallpaper that keeps the graphics chip busy stops it from dropping into its low power states, and that can cut noticeably into unplugged time. Heat and fan noise follow from the same thing.

The settings that matter

  1. Frame rate. A wallpaper does not need to match your monitor's refresh rate. 30 frames per second looks smooth for slow, drifting motion and halves the work of 60. Choose 60 when you want fast motion to look silky and you have the headroom.
  2. Pause when covered. The single most important setting. When a full screen app, a game or a maximised window covers the desktop, the wallpaper should stop drawing completely, not just slow down.
  3. Quality or detail. Lower detail settings draw fewer elements or at a lower internal resolution. On a 4K screen this is often the difference between a light load and a heavy one.
  4. A calm or still mode. A slower motion mode, or a way to freeze the scene entirely, is useful on battery, during calls, or when you want the look without the movement.

Laptops, 4K screens and several monitors

On a laptop, start at 30 frames per second with the calm mode on, and check battery life over a normal day before turning things up. On a 4K screen, prefer apps that render at the screen's real resolution rather than stretching a smaller image, because a stretched wallpaper looks soft however good the original is. With several monitors, each screen is drawn separately, so the cost adds up; it is worth checking Task Manager again after adding a second display.

Where to get them safely

Wallpaper packs and players passed around as downloads from unfamiliar sites are a common way to pick up unwanted software, because the installer runs with your permissions. Apps from the Microsoft Store are packaged, checked before release, install into their own space and remove cleanly when you uninstall them, which makes the Store the safer place to look.
